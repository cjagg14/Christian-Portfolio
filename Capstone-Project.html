Project Description

AER813 — Space Systems Design Project is the final-year capstone for the aerospace engineering program at Toronto Metropolitan University. Our team is designing the SOOS-1 (Satellite On-Orbit Servicing) spacecraft: an autonomous mothership that launches to geostationary orbit, rendezvous with five defunct commercial satellites, docks with each one via a universal engine-bell capture mechanism, and either restores them to their operational GEO slot or passivates them into a graveyard orbit.

The mission targets five real satellites — INTELSAT-603, ITALSAT-2, THAICOM-1, GOES-1, and HISPSAT-1C — each drifting in different graveyard inclinations between 4.87° and 13.74°. The spacecraft carries detachable AOC modules that remain permanently attached to each serviced satellite to provide attitude and stationkeeping control for the remainder of its renewed life.

Mission Animation

The mission animation was produced in Blender, driven by trajectory data exported from ANSYS STK. The sequence depicts the full rendezvous profile — from the mothership's 28.5° inclined launch trajectory through the first docking with INTELSAT-603 at graveyard orbit, with each subsequent capture shown in order. Booster separation, transfer burns, and proximity operations are all visualised in the animation.

Orbital Design — STK

Using ANSYS STK, I modelled the orbital trajectories for the complete servicing sequence. Each of the five target satellites occupies a unique graveyard orbit with its own inclination, RAAN, and longitude — making the path between them a constrained multi-stop optimisation problem. STK was used to propagate the orbits to year 2035 and simulate each rendezvous, producing the delta-V and fuel consumption figures verified against the MATLAB results.

Ground station access times were also simulated using NASA's three Deep Space Network antennas (Goldstone, Madrid, Canberra), confirming continuous communication coverage is maintained throughout the mission profile.

ΔV & Fuel Optimisation — MATLAB

I wrote a constrained Travelling Salesman Problem solver in MATLAB to find the optimal satellite visiting order. The algorithm builds a cost matrix from both plane-change ΔV (a function of relative inclination and RAAN) and longitude phasing ΔV for each possible inter-satellite transfer, then exhaustively searches all permutations of the middle three targets — with INTELSAT-603 fixed first to shed module mass early, and HISPSAT-1C fixed last due to design constraints.

This approach reduced total mission fuel from 11.9 T (unconstrained order) to 10.6 T, freeing over 1,300 kg of mass allocation for other subsystems. The full velocity and fuel requirements for each leg were then validated in STK, with results agreeing closely with the MATLAB model.

Mission Visualisation — Blender

The complete mission sequence was animated in Blender using keyframe data derived directly from the STK trajectory outputs. The scene includes a hierarchical spacecraft rig — a Launch_Vehicle parent object with the mothership and booster stages as children — with tangent-aligned rotation applied through quaternion composition to keep the spacecraft oriented along its velocity vector at all times.

Booster separation is handled via a Child Of constraint with a gradual influence fade, smoothly detaching the stage at the correct mission time. A chase camera with a local offset tracks the mothership throughout, and the closest approach to INTELSAT-603 at approximately 26.6 hours is captured in detail.

Team

The project is a collaboration between Evan Aceti, Giuseppe Botta, Kyle Clark, Christian Jaggard, and Elbron Merza, supervised by Dr. Krishna Kumar. Task responsibilities are divided across orbital path determination and STK simulations, attitude control system design, communications subsystem design, and demonstrator hardware development. The Critical Design Review was submitted in March 2026 and the project continues toward the Flight Readiness Review.
